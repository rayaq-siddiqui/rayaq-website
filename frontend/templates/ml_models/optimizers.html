Optimizers: SGD to AdamW

Backpropagation gives the gradient; the optimizer decides what to do with it. This page builds up from plain stochastic gradient descent through momentum, Nesterov and RMSProp to Adam and AdamW, races them on the same narrow valley, works one Adam step through by hand, shows why decoupled weight decay is not the same as L2 regularization, and reads the update rules out of torch.optim at {{ upstream.tag }}.

Problem

Gradient descent says: move every parameter a small step against its gradient, θ ← θ − η∇L(θ). Two things make that harder than it sounds. First, the true gradient is an average over the whole training set, and computing it for every step would be far too slow, so every step uses a noisy estimate from a small batch. Second, the loss surface is badly shaped. In some directions it is steep, in others nearly flat, and one learning rate η has to serve all of them: small enough not to blow up in the steep directions, which makes it crawl in the flat ones.

An optimizer is a rule for turning the stream of noisy gradients into steps that make fast, stable progress anyway. The rules on this page differ in what they remember between steps, and that memory (the optimizer's state) costs real memory: for Adam, two extra numbers per parameter.

Intuition

Picture a long, narrow valley: gentle along its floor, steep across it. Plain gradient descent points mostly across the valley, because that's where the slope is, so it bounces from wall to wall and creeps along the floor. Raise the learning rate to creep faster and the bouncing grows until it diverges.

  • Momentum keeps a running sum of past gradients, like a heavy ball. The across-the-valley components alternate in sign and cancel; the along-the-floor components agree and add up, so the ball builds speed where it should.
  • RMSProp divides each parameter's gradient by a running average of its own size. The steep direction, with big gradients, gets a small step; the flat direction gets a big one. Every parameter gets its own effective learning rate.
  • Adam does both: a running average of the gradient (momentum) divided by a running average of its square (RMSProp), plus a correction for the averages starting at zero. Its steps are about η in size whatever the gradient's scale.
  • AdamW is Adam with weight decay applied directly to the weights rather than folded into the gradient, where Adam's division would distort it.

Mechanics

SGD and minibatches

The training loss is an average over N examples, L(θ) = (1/N) Σ ℓi(θ). Stochastic gradient descent replaces its gradient with the average over a random minibatch B of B examples:

g = (1/B) Σi∈B ∇ℓi(θ),   θ ← θ − η g

g is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient: right on average, with noise whose standard deviation falls like 1/√B. Quadrupling the batch halves the noise but costs four times the compute per step, which is why moderate batches win on total compute. The noise isn't purely harmful either; it helps escape saddle points and is thought to favour flatter minima that generalize better. Batch size and learning rate interact: bigger batches allow bigger η, roughly in proportion for SGD, up to a limit.

On a quadratic, the step along a direction with curvature a multiplies the error by (1 − ηa). That is stable only while |1 − ηa| < 1, that is η < 2/a, so the steepest direction sets the largest usable learning rate, and the flattest direction then sets how slowly everything converges. In the valley above, a = {{ n(bowl.curvatures[1]) }} across it caps η at {{ n(2 / bowl.curvatures[1]) }}; at η = {{ n(race[0].hyper.lr) }} the x error shrinks by only {{ n(1 - race[0].hyper.lr * bowl.curvatures[0]) }} per step. The ratio of the largest curvature to the smallest, the condition number, is the core difficulty every rule below attacks.

Momentum and Nesterov

Momentum keeps a velocity buffer b and steps along it:

b ← μb + g,   θ ← θ − η b

With μ = 0.9, a gradient that keeps the same sign is added up to an effective 1/(1 − μ) = 10 times; one that alternates mostly cancels. That is exactly the valley's problem: the floor direction is consistent, the wall-to-wall direction alternates. PyTorch's SGD stores b as momentum_buffer, initializes it to the first gradient, and (with the default dampening=0) uses exactly this form; some texts write b ← μb + (1 − μ)g instead, which only rescales η.

Nesterov momentum steps along g + μb instead of b: it applies the momentum "look-ahead" to the current gradient as well, which reacts sooner when the velocity starts carrying the parameters uphill. It is a small change with a better worst-case theory for convex problems. Note what it does to the step on the fresh gradient: η(1 + μ) instead of η, so it needs a smaller learning rate. In the race, Nesterov at η = 0.03 diverges, while at η = 0.02 it reaches {{ n(race[3].final) }}.

RMSProp

RMSProp keeps a running average of each parameter's squared gradient and divides by its root:

s ← αs + (1 − α)g²,   θ ← θ − η g / (√s + ε)

Everything is element-wise, so each parameter gets its own scale. If a parameter's gradients are consistently around size 50, √s ≈ 50 and the step is about η; if they're around 1, the step is also about η. That flattens the valley's condition number for any problem whose steep directions line up with the parameter axes. PyTorch's RMSprop defaults to α = 0.99, ε = 10−8 and stores s as square_avg. Its ancestor AdaGrad summed g² over all time instead of averaging, so its steps shrank forever; the moving average is what lets RMSProp keep learning.

Adam: moment estimates and bias correction

Adam keeps both averages, called the first and second moment estimates:

m ← β1m + (1 − β1)g,   v ← β2v + (1 − β2)g²

m̂ = m / (1 − β1t),   v̂ = v / (1 − β2t),   θ ← θ − η m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)

with defaults η = 10−3, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ε = 10−8. m is momentum and v is RMSProp's average, so the step m̂/√v̂ is roughly "the gradient's sign, smoothed", and its size is at most about η per parameter.

The hats are the bias correction. Both averages start at 0, so early on they are too small: after one step m = (1 − β1)g = 0.1g and v = 0.001g². Dividing by 1 − βt rescales them to unbiased estimates of the gradient's mean and mean square. Without the correction, the first step would be η · 0.1g / √(0.001g²) = {{ n(uncorrected_step_ratio(ax.betas, 1)) }}η, and because v's average is much slower than m's, the overshoot gets worse before it gets better, peaking near t = 12.

For a constant gradient, the corrected step is exactly η from step 1. The uncorrected step starts {{ "%.2f"|format(uncorrected_step_ratio(ax.betas, 1)) }}× too large, reaches {{ "%.2f"|format(uncorrected_step_ratio(ax.betas, 12)) }}× at t = 12, and is still {{ "%.2f"|format(uncorrected_step_ratio(ax.betas, 1000)) }}× at t = 1000. Early steps are exactly when the parameters are worst, which is why learning-rate warmup is often used on top.

Because the step is normalized, Adam is far less sensitive to the gradient's scale than SGD, and one learning rate works across layers whose gradients differ by orders of magnitude. That, plus robustness to noisy and sparse gradients, is why it became the default for transformers. It is not a free win: on some problems, especially image classification with well-tuned schedules, SGD with momentum still generalizes slightly better.

AdamW: decoupled weight decay

Weight decay shrinks every weight toward zero each step. For SGD, adding an L2 penalty (λ/2)‖θ‖² to the loss does exactly that: the gradient gains a term λθ, and the step −η(g + λθ) is the usual step plus θ ← (1 − ηλ)θ. The two ideas coincide, and SGD(weight_decay=λ) implements decay by adding λθ to the gradient.

Adam breaks the equivalence. Adam(weight_decay=λ) also adds λθ to the gradient, and then divides by √v̂. A weight whose gradients are large gets its decay divided down to almost nothing; a weight with tiny gradients gets its decay amplified. Loshchilov and Hutter's AdamW decouples the two: it shrinks the weight directly, θ ← (1 − ηλ)θ, and then takes an ordinary Adam step on the unmodified gradient. In PyTorch that is the single line param.mul_(1 - lr * weight_decay), and AdamW is Adam with decoupled_weight_decay=True and a default λ of 10−2 instead of 0.

AdamW's two curves lie on top of each other: {{ n(decay[2]["values"][-1]) }} after {{ wd.steps }} steps either way, matching (1 − ηλ){{ wd.steps }} = {{ n((1 - wd.lr * wd.weight_decay) ** wd.steps) }}.

Optimizer state memory

Every bit of memory an optimizer keeps is per parameter. For a model with 7 billion parameters in float32:

The weights themselves are another 28 GB and the gradients another 28 GB, so Adam training needs 16 bytes per parameter, 112 GB at 7B, before a single activation is stored. Mixed-precision training usually keeps the same total: bfloat16 weights and gradients (4 bytes) plus a float32 master copy and Adam's two moments (12 bytes). This is why optimizer state is the first thing sharded across GPUs in large-model training (ZeRO and FSDP), and why memory-lean variants exist: Adafactor factorizes v into row and column averages, and 8-bit Adam quantizes both moments. PyTorch also keeps a tiny step counter per parameter.

In PyTorch

Every optimizer subclasses torch.optim.Optimizer. It is built from an iterable of parameters (or of parameter groups, dicts that each carry their own lr, weight_decay and so on), keeps self.param_groups and a self.state dict keyed by parameter, and exposes three calls a training loop uses:

  • zero_grad() clears every parameter's .grad, by default by setting it to None, because backward accumulates;
  • step() reads each .grad, updates the state and changes the parameters in place, under torch.no_grad();
  • state_dict() / load_state_dict() save and restore the state, so a resumed run continues with the same moments rather than restarting Adam's warm-up.

Adam.step gathers the tensors of each group and calls a functional adam, which dispatches to one of three implementations: _single_tensor_adam, a plain loop over parameters that is easiest to read; a foreach version that batches the same operations over lists of tensors; and a fused kernel that does the whole update in one GPU launch. The default on GPU is foreach. They compute the same update.

theta = torch.tensor([0.5, -0.3], requires_grad=True)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([theta], lr=1e-3, betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8)
for g in ([0.02, -4.0], [0.01, -3.0], [0.03, -5.0]):
    opt.zero_grad()
    theta.grad = torch.tensor(g)        # stand-in for loss.backward()

Every rule on this page, in plain Python:

import math

def step(kind, params, grads, state, lr, mu=0.9, alpha=0.99,
         betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8, wd=0.0):
    t = state["t"] = state.get("t", 0) + 1
    if kind == "adamw":
        params = [p * (1 - lr * wd) for p in params]
    else:
        grads = [g + wd * p for g, p in zip(grads, params)]
    if kind == "sgd":
        return [p - lr * g for p, g in zip(params, grads)]
    if kind in ("momentum", "nesterov"):
        buf = state["buf"] = grads if t == 1 else [mu * b + g for b, g in zip(state["buf"], grads)]
        d = buf if kind == "momentum" else [g + mu * b for g, b in zip(grads, buf)]
        return [p - lr * x for p, x in zip(params, d)]
    if kind == "rmsprop":
        s = state["s"] = [alpha * s + (1 - alpha) * g * g
                          for s, g in zip(state.get("s", [0.0] * len(grads)), grads)]
        return [p - lr * g / (math.sqrt(v) + eps) for p, g, v in zip(params, grads, s)]
    b1, b2 = betas
    m = state["m"] = [b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g for m, g in zip(state.get("m", [0.0] * len(grads)), grads)]
    v = state["v"] = [b2 * v + (1 - b2) * g * g for v, g in zip(state.get("v", [0.0] * len(grads)), grads)]
    return [p - lr * (mi / (1 - b1**t)) / (math.sqrt(vi / (1 - b2**t)) + eps)
            for p, mi, vi in zip(params, m, v)]

theta, state = [0.5, -0.3], {}
for g in ([0.02, -4.0], [0.01, -3.0], [0.03, -5.0]):
    theta = step("adam", theta, g, state, lr=1e-3)
theta                                   # [0.497125..., -0.297029...]

Tradeoffs

  • Adaptive versus plain. Adam and AdamW need little tuning and handle badly scaled and sparse gradients; they're the default for transformers. SGD with momentum uses half the state memory and, with a carefully tuned schedule, often generalizes a little better on vision tasks.
  • Memory. Adam doubles the parameter memory again, 8 extra bytes per float32 parameter; at billions of parameters that drives sharding, 8-bit states or factored variants like Adafactor.
  • Hyperparameters still matter. The learning rate dominates everything else. For large models β2 = 0.95 is common, because 0.999 averages over about a thousand steps and reacts too slowly when gradients spike; ε matters in low precision, where 10−8 underflows in float16.
  • L2 or decoupled decay. With SGD they're the same. With Adam use AdamW, whose decay strength doesn't depend on the gradient scale, and usually exclude biases and normalization weights from decay.
  • Implementation. foreach and fused versions compute the same update with far fewer kernel launches; the single-tensor loop exists for clarity and for unusual tensor types.

Check yourself

On a quadratic with curvatures 1 and 50, what's the largest learning rate plain gradient descent can use?

Just under 2/50 = 0.04. The steep direction diverges above it, and at that rate the flat direction shrinks its error by only 4% per step.

Adam's first step for a parameter with gradient g = −4, and one with g = 0.02, both at η = 10−3?

+0.001 and −0.001. With bias correction m̂ = g and v̂ = g², so the step is −η·sign(g): the size of the gradient doesn't matter at all on step 1.

Without bias correction, would Adam's first steps be too large or too small, and by roughly how much?

Too large: m is 0.1g but √v is only √0.001·|g| ≈ 0.032|g|, so the step is about 3.2η at t = 1, and the gap grows to about 6.6η around t = 12 before shrinking.

Why is Adam(weight_decay=0.01) not the same as AdamW(weight_decay=0.01)?

Adam adds λθ to the gradient and then divides by √v̂, so the decay is scaled down for weights with large gradients and up for ones with small gradients. AdamW multiplies the weight by (1 − ηλ) directly, the same for every weight.

You resume training from a checkpoint that saved the model but not the optimizer. What changes?

Adam's m, v and step count restart at zero, so the bias correction starts over and the first steps behave like the start of training, often causing a loss spike. Save opt.state_dict() too.
