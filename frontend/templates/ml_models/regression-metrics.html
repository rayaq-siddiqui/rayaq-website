Regression metrics

A regression model outputs a number, and a metric turns a list of misses into one score. Different metrics reward different models: one punishes the rare huge miss, another the many small ones, a third only asks whether the predictions move with the target. This page computes MAE, RMSE, median absolute error, R², explained variance, MAPE and the pinball loss on one small dataset, shows the ranking flip between them, and ties each metric to the single prediction it rewards.

Problem

A house-price model, a delivery-time model and a demand forecast each produce a column of predictions next to a column of true values. To compare two models, tune hyperparameters or decide whether a model is good enough to ship, those two columns have to become one number. There are many ways to do that, and they disagree. A model can win on mean absolute error and lose on root mean squared error, look strong on R² for one test set and weak on another with the same errors, or score perfectly on explained variance while every prediction is off by the same amount.

The choice is not cosmetic. Each metric is minimised by a different prediction, so picking the metric picks what the model is pushed towards, the mean, the median or a quantile. The question to ask is what a miss costs the people who use the prediction.

Intuition

Every metric on this page starts from the residual r = y − ŷ and applies a penalty to it. Squaring makes a miss of 4 cost sixteen times a miss of 1, so one big miss can dominate the score. The absolute value treats every unit of error the same. The pinball loss charges the two sides differently, so under-predicting can be made nine times as costly as over-predicting. The penalty shape is also what makes each metric prefer a particular summary of the data, the same link the loss functions page draws for training.

Penalty per residual. Positive r means the prediction was too low. The pinball line is steep on the right and nearly flat on the left.

Mechanics

Error metrics in the target's units

MAE = (1/N) Σ |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|    MSE = (1/N) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²    RMSE = √MSE
MedAE = median |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|    max error = max |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|

MAE and RMSE are in the same units as the target, so "off by 2.9 on average" is something a stakeholder can read. RMSE is never smaller than MAE, and the gap between them grows with how uneven the errors are: equal-sized misses give RMSE = MAE, one large miss among small ones gives RMSE far above MAE. The median absolute error ignores the largest half of the misses entirely, and the max error looks only at the worst one. Lower is better for all of them, and none has a natural scale, so a value means something only next to a baseline.

R² and explained variance

R² = 1 − Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² / Σ (yᵢ − ȳ)²    explained variance = 1 − Var(y − ŷ) / Var(y)

R², the coefficient of determination, compares the model's squared error with that of the best constant prediction, the mean of the test targets. A score of 1 is perfect, 0 means no better than predicting ȳ for every row, and anything worse than that is negative; there is no lower bound. Because the denominator is the spread of the test targets, the same errors give a lower R² on a test set whose targets vary less. R² is a property of the model and the data together, not of the model alone.

Explained variance subtracts the mean residual before squaring, so a constant offset costs nothing: a model that is always 5 too high but moves exactly with the target scores 1. When the mean residual is zero the two scores are equal, which is why they agree on most models.

Percentage errors

MAPE = (1/N) Σ |yᵢ − ŷᵢ| / max(|yᵢ|, ε)

MAPE makes errors comparable across series of different sizes, but it divides by the target, which has two side effects. Targets near zero blow it up (scikit-learn's ε is the float64 machine epsilon, so a zero target gives an enormous but finite term). And it is not symmetric: predicting 150 when the truth is 100 costs {{ pct(ex.over_under.over) }}, while predicting 100 when the truth is 150 costs {{ pct(ex.over_under.under) }}. For positive targets and predictions, an under-prediction can cost at most 100%, while an over-prediction has no limit, so a model tuned for MAPE learns to aim low. scikit-learn returns MAPE as a fraction, not multiplied by 100.

Mean squared log error, (1/N) Σ (log(1 + yᵢ) − log(1 + ŷᵢ))², is the other relative metric: it scores ratios rather than differences and is meant for targets that grow exponentially. scikit-learn computes it with log1p and raises a ValueError for any value at or below −1, where the log is undefined.

Pinball (quantile) loss

pinballα(y, ŷ) = α (y − ŷ) if y ≥ ŷ, otherwise (1 − α)(ŷ − y)

The pinball loss is the asymmetric absolute error behind quantile regression (Koenker and Bassett, 1978). With α = 0.5 it is half the absolute error. With α = 0.9 a miss below the target costs 0.9 per unit and a miss above costs 0.1, so the best prediction sits where about 90% of the targets are below it: the 0.9 quantile. It is the metric for a prediction that is meant to be an upper or lower bound, such as stock to hold so that demand is covered nine days in ten.

What each metric rewards

The cleanest way to see what a metric wants is to ask which single number c, predicted for every row, minimises it. Squared error is minimised by the mean, absolute error by any median, the pinball loss by the α-quantile. Gneiting (2011) makes the general point: a point forecast should be scored with a function that is consistent for the summary it is meant to report, the mean for squared error and quantiles for pinball losses. Scoring a median forecast with RMSE, or a mean forecast with MAE, penalises the model for doing what it was asked.

A model's R² on the test set is −0.3. What does that say?

Its squared error is 30% larger than that of predicting the test set's own mean for every row. That is worse than the simplest baseline, often a sign of a distribution shift between training and test data, a broken pipeline, or a model that extrapolates badly.

Implementation

from sklearn.metrics import (
    mean_absolute_error, root_mean_squared_error, median_absolute_error,
    max_error, mean_absolute_percentage_error, r2_score,
    explained_variance_score, mean_pinball_loss,
)

y_true = [12, 14, 15, 15, 17, 18, 20, 22, 25, 31, 38, 55]
y_pred = [12.5, 13.5, 15, 15.5, 16.5, 18, 20.5, 21.5, 25, 31.5, 37.5, 35]

for name, metric in [
    ("MAE", mean_absolute_error),
    ("RMSE", root_mean_squared_error),
    ("MedAE", median_absolute_error),
    ("max", max_error),
    ("MAPE", mean_absolute_percentage_error),
    ("R2", r2_score),
    ("explained var.", explained_variance_score),
]:
    print(f"{name:15s}{metric(y_true, y_pred):.3f}")

print(mean_pinball_loss(y_true, y_pred, alpha=0.9))

# GridSearchCV(..., scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error")

The y_pred above is the spiky model, so the printed values match its row in the worked example. scikit-learn metrics take (y_true, y_pred) in that order, which matters for the asymmetric ones (MAPE, pinball, MSLE) and for R², whose denominator comes from y_true. Scoring strings for search and cross-validation are negated where lower is better, so "higher is better" holds everywhere: neg_mean_absolute_error, neg_root_mean_squared_error, while r2 is used as is.

On the training side, the loss should match the metric where it can: nn.MSELoss for RMSE, nn.L1Loss for MAE, nn.HuberLoss in between, and the pinball loss (a two-line custom loss in PyTorch) for quantiles. Gradient boosting libraries offer squared, absolute and quantile objectives.

Tradeoffs

  • MAE or RMSE. If cost grows with the square of the miss, or the mean is the quantity being forecast, use RMSE. If every unit of error costs the same, or outliers in the target are noise, use MAE. Reporting both shows how uneven the errors are.
  • R² across datasets. R² depends on the test targets' variance, so it cannot compare models evaluated on different data. Compare errors in units, or against a fixed baseline, instead.
  • R² on small or shifted test sets. R² measures against the test set's own mean, which a deployed model never knows. A "model" that predicted that mean would score 0 without having learned anything.
  • Explained variance. It hides a systematic offset. Use it only together with the mean residual, or use R².
  • Percentage errors. MAPE favours under-prediction and breaks near zero. For series compared across scales, MASE (Hyndman and Koehler, 2006; see time series) avoids both problems.
  • Quantiles and intervals. A pinball loss at one α scores one quantile. Two quantile models (say 0.1 and 0.9) give an interval, judged by how often it covers the target and how wide it is.
  • Validation. Any metric is only as good as the split it is computed on; see cross-validation.
A team trains with MSE loss but reports MAE to stakeholders. What can go wrong?

The model is pushed towards the conditional mean while the metric rewards the conditional median. On skewed targets these differ, so a model chosen for the lowest training loss may not have the lowest MAE, and an MAE-trained model may score better on the reported number. Match the training loss, the model-selection metric and the reported metric where you can.
